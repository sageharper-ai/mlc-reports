What this report is (and isn't)

Is: A distillation of what ~10 impact workers said — in chat and on mic — during the 90-minute structured interview session you hosted. Every claim below is either verbatim quote (in italics) or a tight paraphrase traceable to the session transcript and chat log. A separate Synthesis section marks my interpretive layer, so you can accept or reject the pattern reading independently of the data.

Isn't: A product critique of Mito. I don't know Mito well enough yet to critique it. This is the voice of your prospective users, captured before the demo.

The interview questions (as actually asked)

Transcribed from the session, in the order Sage asked them. Questions 3 and 6 were slightly adjusted live, and Q6 was noted during the call as functionally overlapping with the marketing question in Q5.

  1. Give us a quick snapshot. What's your community or organization, and what does collaboration look like on a typical week for you?
  2. When you think about the tools and the system you use to coordinate, what's the feeling that comes to mind? Are you feeling empowered, frustrated, overwhelmed?
  3. Tool stacking gaps. Name the tool or the platform you're using, and where do you feel it's doing a good job, and where do you feel it's falling short?
  4. Strategy alignment. When you're launching a new initiative or partnership, how do you figure out who's doing what? How do you figure out what resources exist? How do you get everyone aligned on the mission? (Including the seven forms of capital.)
  5. Impact and transparency. How do you currently track and share the impact of your work? Is it easy for collaborators and stakeholders to see what you're accomplishing and where you're headed?
  6. Finding your people. How do you find and connect with aligned people in your organization outside of your immediate work? (Sage noted during the call this overlapped with the marketing thread in Q5.)
  7. Knowledge and coordination. When your team or community creates something valuable — a process, a lessons learned, a playbook — how does that knowledge get captured? Do you have a limited commons, open commons, or a way to store these so they don't get lost through conversation?
  8. Requests and needs. When you need something — a skill, a resource, a connection, one of the seven forms of capital — how do you put that need out into your community, and how do people find out what you need?
  9. If you could design the perfect system for how your community could work together — not just task management, but the whole experience of collaboration — what's the one top priority you'd want the system to do for you?
  10. What's the one thing you wish someone would just build already to make community collaboration easier?

Worth holding

Your own live synthesis was accurate. Those four — unheard, redundancy, duplication, trackability — are the pain signatures the room agreed to.

Q3 — Tool stacking gaps

This question surfaced the rawest moment of the call. Colleen returned to it at length during open Q&A:

I hate SaaS. I keep getting billed, billed and billed, and billed and billed for all of these separate systems that don't talk to each other. I'm paying $1,000 a year for something that's just crap now. It used to be an excellent system. It was a wonderful system when I was paying $15 a year for it. It's horrible now that I'm paying $1,000 a year for it.— Colleen Dick

Brian named Monday.com's hierarchy ceiling. Karen named the cost / duplication loop. David named the GRC stack (cheap Google + Slack) as the only thing that actually works at scale for them, but with "no synthesis" of what's inside it. That phrase — no synthesis — became a recurring theme later in the call.

Q4 — Strategy alignment

  • Brian: Sociocratic circle method — "has become natural for me… but that's after 2 years of sociocratic training." The training cliff matters.
  • Colleen: Mondragon governance model — "We've studied Mondragon extensively... their governance model works beautifully. It's got 70 years' worth of experience behind it."
  • Everyone else: Emergent, informal. Karen: "some of it is just practice, which we never really agreed to — it just emerged, and isn't even conscious."
Implication for Mito

The orgs with working alignment have either 2-year training curves or 70-year-old frameworks behind them. Anyone relying on good defaults will drift. If Mito bakes a governance pattern in, make it visible and opt-in — not hidden automation.

Synthesis — four patterns (my interpretive layer)

Four patterns emerged that I'd treat as load-bearing for your roadmap. This is interpretation, not data — take or leave.

1. The "shadow work" problem is the emotional center

Your Martin / Uganda story landed because it named the pain the room was feeling. Your strongest product-market fit signal was not collaboration-as-tasks — it was visibility-as-dignity. The people working hard in obscurity; the donors/allies who can't find them; the layperson (Tally) who wants to plug into one cause without joining yet another chat.

If Mito has a "stream of good work happening, attributable to specific humans" feature, that's where the oxygen is.

2. Memory gardening is the infrastructure problem no one has solved

Brian named it. Karen named it. Emmanuelle named it from a different angle ("weaving journal, not Slack"). Rick named it ("curate content, cut the crap"). Every participant described a personal-brain-as-bottleneck. Nobody has working organizational memory — the best available is a 3-year rolling notes doc that drops items when they fall off.

If Mito has, or plans, AI-assisted memory gardening — not another RAG lookup, but a reconcile / supersede / deprecate workflow — that's the moat. Ship with a human gardener role visible, because no one trusts silent AI with their history.

3. Free tier is load-bearing for adoption

Three separate participants (Karen, Colleen, Emmanuelle) explicitly said they walked away from paid tools. You named it yourself: "All great things start with a conversation, and so if you're stifling the conversation, you're directly impeding the innovation that needs to happen." Hold this. The moment pricing hits the circle, adoption dies.

Your instinct to split free-community from paid-admin-workspace maps to what Emmanuelle described: "closed doors, and this team would meet... I don't think that everybody needs to know about everything." That's real. Keep it.

4. The 10-person cliff is real

Multiple participants, without coordinating, said chats over ~10 people lose engagement. Nikki: "Too many people NOT checking in on Slack." You: "Once the number gets past 10, I think people check out."

If Mito scales messaging beyond 10 without a structural intervention (threads? summaries? accountability surfacing?) you inherit Slack's failure mode.

What I'd probe on before the Wednesday demo

Grounded questions this session didn't resolve that would materially shape your roadmap:

  1. Who pays and when? Everyone said free is load-bearing. How does Mito monetize without hitting the Karen-at-CO2 wall? Per-admin? Per-workspace? Impact-revenue share? This shapes the whole architecture.
  2. Memory-gardening UI. Brian is literally building something in this space. He's a potential design partner, a potential competitor, or both. Worth a direct 1:1 — he offered one on the call: "Yeah, pleasure, I'll book some time with you, we can talk about context and memory."
  3. What's the non-SaaS frame? Colleen's rant about 17 disconnected tools was the loudest moment of the call. If you position Mito as "not another SaaS" — what's the positive frame? Platform, protocol, commons, utility? The framing affects willingness-to-pay.
  4. The visibility / dignity feature. Martin's story moved the room. Does Mito actually ship a "human-behind-the-work" surface, or is that v2? If v2, flag it Wednesday — people will project that need onto whatever you show.
  5. The 10-person cliff. What's Mito's structural intervention for scaling group comms past 10 without the Slack-scroll death spiral?

One honest caveat

Sample bias

This was a 90-minute structured interview with a self-selecting GRC subset. They are not a random sample of your TAM — they are people who show up on Wednesdays at 11 AM ET to talk about regeneration. That's a specific psychographic (mission-driven, relational, anti-SaaS, under-resourced, technically curious).

Treat this report as signal from the regenerative ecosystem, not signal from all impact workers. If Mito's target is broader — small business owners, traditional nonprofits, commercial teams — run this interview again with a different room and expect different answers on pricing tolerance, governance appetite, and memory-gardening priority.
