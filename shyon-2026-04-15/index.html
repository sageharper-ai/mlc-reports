What this report is (and isn't)

Is: A distillation of what ~10 impact workers said during the session you hosted, cross-checked against current external data so each finding has two layers — what the room said and what the broader research says. Prep material for the Wednesday follow-up, where you'll be the one answering the same questions they did.

Isn't: A product critique of Mito. I don't know Mito well enough yet. This is the voice of your prospective users, plus the market evidence, so you can walk in Wednesday knowing which of their answers are GRC-specific and which map onto systemic market realities.

Wednesday's role in the iteration

This is not a demo or a pitch. Sage framed it to the room verbatim: "we're gonna run through the exact same questions again, but we're asking Cheyenne next week. We're asking how he's planning on doing this. And he's gonna be very honest, because he doesn't have all of this worked out."

That's the structural integrity of the series. The group answered ten questions about how they collaborate today and where it falls short. Wednesday, you answer the same ten questions — not about your current practice, but about how Mito is being built to respond to each pain. Same seat, same prompts, same rules: concise answers, transparent about the state of each answer (shipped, in-progress, roadmap, unknown). Specifically no selling.

This report is calibration material for that moment. Each finding below maps back to one or two of those ten questions so you can see, before you sit down, where the room's pain was loudest, what external data says about whether that pain is systemic, and what answer Wednesday is going to need from you.

Method — how evidence is layered

Each of the seven findings below follows the same structure:

  • Question — which of the ten session questions surfaced this pattern.
  • Finding — what participants actually said, with verbatim quotes traceable to the transcript and chat log.
  • Supporting data — cross-checked against published research, industry reports, academic studies, and vendor-neutral benchmarks from 2023 onward. Every claim is URL-cited; no unsourced numbers.
  • Verdict — whether this is a niche quirk of the GRC audience, or a systemic pattern documented across the broader market.

The data and the interpretation are decoupled on purpose: you can accept the data even if you reject the interpretation, or vice versa. Nothing is load-bearing on anything else.

Findings — seven patterns with supporting data

From Q1 + Q3

1. Tool sprawl & paid-tool abandonment

Finding: Three participants (Karen, Emmanuelle, Colleen) independently said they walked away from paid project-management tools because the economics didn't work and their teams duplicated in informal channels anyway. Colleen's rant — "I hate SaaS. I keep getting billed and billed for all of these separate systems that don't talk to each other" — was the loudest moment of the call.

Used to use Asana and Basecamp… it costs money we don't have, and also everybody's using other things, and so having one project where you're using the thing that costs money, that's very specialized — it just wasn't worth it.— Karen Wolfgang, CO2 Foundation
We theoretically use Monday.com but they jerked us around on prices in the past and it has limited hierarchy — subtasks of subtasks.— Dr. Brian von Herzen
Supporting data
  • 72% of nonprofits use three or more systems that don't talk to each other; 55% say their systems aren't aligned to their workflow. NTEN 2022 Data Empowerment Report
  • 77% of nonprofits cite lack of budget as the top obstacle to tech investment; 45% say they aren't spending enough on tech. NTEN 2024 Nonprofit Digital Investments Report
  • Most nonprofits spend under 3% of their budget on tech; only 13% spend over 5%; 62% say they lack time to vet and implement new tools. Chronicle of Philanthropy, 2024
  • The average company now runs 101 apps, up from 93 the year prior. Okta Businesses at Work 2025
  • At small companies, shadow IT makes up 68% of the app portfolio (vs. 52% at mid/large). Shadow apps see 54% engagement vs. 40% for IT-sanctioned tools — meaning the unofficial channels actually get more use than the paid stack. Productiv State of SaaS 2024
Verdict — systemic

Colleen's "17 disconnected tools" rant is a documented systemic pattern, not an audience quirk. Small orgs consistently under-fund tech, run 3+ disconnected systems, and run majority-shadow-IT portfolios where unofficial tools outperform the paid stack on actual usage. Any product positioned as "another paid SaaS" walks into this wall.

From Q2 + Q9 + Q10

2. The ~10-person group-size engagement cliff

Finding: Multiple participants independently described chats over roughly ten people as non-functional. Sage named it during the call: "once the number gets past 10, I think people check out." Nikki: "Too many people NOT checking in on slack." David: "nobody listens to everything, lots of people don't listen to anything."

Supporting data
  • Dunbar hierarchy (Zhou, Sornette, Hill, Dunbar, 2005, Proc. Royal Society B): discrete social layers at ~5, 15, 50, 150 with scaling ratio ~3. The 15-person "sympathy group" is the outer edge of high-trust contact; beyond it, emotional closeness and contact frequency drop sharply. PubMed
  • Amazon two-pizza / Staats–Milkman–Fox (2012): 2-person teams built a Lego figure in 36 min; 4-person teams took 52 min (44% longer) and systematically under-estimated completion time as size grew. Coordination cost rises non-linearly past ~5–8. AWS
  • Nielsen 90-9-1 participation inequality (2006): in online communities, 90% lurk, 9% contribute occasionally, 1% produce most content. The participation drop is structural, not a function of channel quality. Nielsen Norman Group
  • Slack channel benchmarks: general channels average ~80% lurkers; "healthy" engagement target is 25%, observed is often ~10%; 3–5% of users drive most collaboration. Count.co
Verdict — directionally systemic

The exact number "10" is not a named constant in the literature, but it sits between Dunbar's 5–15 sympathy layer and the two-pizza 7±2 productive-meeting size — both of which predict engagement collapse as groups cross ~10. Four independent research traditions (evolutionary anthropology, HCI, organizational psychology, platform analytics) converge on the same shape. The room's anecdote is matched.

From Q7 + Q9 + Q10

3. Organizational memory loss & "memory gardening"

Finding: Every participant described a version of the same pain — knowledge lives in people's heads and rolling notes docs, and "if something falls off, it falls off, oops." Brian named it in chat: "How are memories deprecated, revised, prioritized, conflict-resolved, and sorted properly? This memory gardening is essential to building coherent context for orgs around the world." When Sage asked "how many of you feel like you're a bottleneck because you are managing things in your head?" — near-universal yes.

I have a very good memory for some projects, so it's all in my mind… I'm looking for a weaving kind of journal — not Slack — where it becomes something… an old-fashioned scrapbook kind of journal.— Emmanuelle
GRC has slack (all history), video recordings, email archives. No synthesis.— David Witzel
Supporting data — the cost of knowledge loss
  • 42% of institutional knowledge is unique to the individual holding it; employees spend 5.3 hrs/week waiting on colleagues for info; inefficient knowledge-sharing costs a 1,000-person company ~$2.7M/yr. Panopto Workplace Knowledge & Productivity Report
  • Knowledge workers spend ~2.5 hrs/day (≈30% of workday) searching; workers find what they need only ~50% of the time. IDC
  • Nonprofit turnover is ~21%; in fall 2024, 67% of nonprofit employees said they'd job-hunt within a year; top-cited consequence was "loss of institutional knowledge." Candid, 2024
Supporting data — state of AI "memory gardening"
  • Zep / Graphiti (temporal knowledge graph, Jan 2025): compares new edges to existing ones, invalidates contradictions via temporal validity. Scored 63.8% on LongMemEval vs. Mem0's 49%. arXiv 2501.13956
  • Mem0 / Mem0g (Apr 2025, ECAI 2025): conflict detector flags contradictions before graph-write; shipping as production. arXiv 2504.19413
  • Multi-source reconciliation is still frontier research: MADAM-RAG, ICR, TCR (2025) show 11–16% gains on contradiction benchmarks — active work, not solved infrastructure. arXiv 2504.13079
Verdict — systemic + open problem

Organizational memory loss is a decade-documented, multi-million-dollar problem — not niche. AI "memory gardening" is early-production for single-agent memory (Zep, Mem0 shipping) but still research-phase for multi-source organizational reconciliation. Brian's framing aligns with an open problem vendors are racing to commercialize. Wednesday — if Mito touches this, say clearly which half: single-agent or multi-source.

From Q2

4. "Unheard" as a measurable state

Finding: When asked what feeling came to mind about current tools, the first chat response was one word: "Unheard." It resonated widely. Karen: "Always feel like I am missing something. Always both hope and unease." Brian: "Project management is top-down — we don't achieve consent of the governed with those tools."

Supporting data
  • 48% of employees and 52% of leaders say their work feels "chaotic and fragmented." 85% of emails are read in under 15 seconds. Workers are interrupted every 2 minutes; 68% lack uninterrupted focus time. Microsoft Work Trend Index 2024/2025
  • Slack processes 1.5B+ messages/day; average user receives 200+ notifications/day. 60% of knowledge workers report high stress/burnout specifically from digital-communication fatigue. Brosix, 2024
  • Frazier et al. (2017) meta-analysis in Personnel Psychology — 136 samples, 22,000+ individuals, 5,000+ groups. Psychological safety (the core construct governing whether people feel heard) correlates strongly with engagement, commitment, and task performance. Wiley
  • Global employee engagement fell from 23% to 21% — sharpest drop since COVID; $438B productivity cost in 2024 alone. Gallup State of the Global Workplace, 2025
  • In the volunteer segment specifically, 46.8% of nonprofit CEOs call volunteer recruitment a "big problem" — a 62% increase since 2003; research flags role ambiguity and communication breakdowns as primary disengagement drivers. Do Good Institute, 2023
Verdict — rigorously systemic

"Unheard" is not a vibes-complaint. It sits at the intersection of measurable message-absorption collapse, channel sprawl, and voice-suppression — three decades of peer-reviewed evidence (Edmondson, Frazier meta-analysis) and annual enterprise data (Microsoft, Gallup) back it. This is the single most-documented finding in the report.

From open-floor + host reframe

5. Free-tier is load-bearing for the impact segment

Finding: Karen: "We have no budget for that group." Emmanuelle ran a grant application off "just Signal and google drive" because it was free. Shyon named it explicitly on the call: "if you're stifling the conversation, you're directly impeding the innovation that needs to happen."

Supporting data
  • ~60% of US nonprofits have annual budgets under $250,000; ~59% have budgets under $50,000; 88% operate under $500,000/year. Candid, 2023 Karen's 5-person volunteer group sits inside this dominant invisible-majority segment.
  • 77% cite budget as the #1 obstacle to tech investment. 92% of the sector is small orgs. NTEN, 2024
  • TechSoup has served 1.06M NGOs with $11.1B in donated products over 30 years; in FY22 alone, Microsoft donated 1.4M licenses to 63,000 nonprofits in 126 countries. TechSoup 2022 Year in Review The entire segment runs on donated/free SaaS because paid-only unit economics don't pencil.
Verdict — your instinct matches the economics

A paid-only gate would exclude the ~60% of US nonprofits under $250K and the ~92% "small org" segment that defines this market. Shyon's instinct to split free community from paid admin workspace maps directly to the documented economics. The hard question is just where the admin-workspace line sits — who pays, and when.

From Q5 + open-floor Martin/Uganda story

6. Visibility-as-dignity — the donor-beneficiary gap

Finding: The emotional center of the call was Shyon's Martin-in-Uganda story: "Nobody will ever be able to see the great work that we're doing here in Africa." Tally mirrored it instantly. Colleen added the donor side: "I get 3–4 daily outreach from 'agents of wealthy people' but I have no way to verify who's real." Bilateral pain.

Supporting data
  • 67% of Americans say trusting a charity is "highly important" before giving, but only 22% "highly trust" charities. The share deterred by not knowing what the charity will do with the money jumped from 24.9% to 33.8% over three years. Visible accomplishments rank #1 as the trust driver. BBB Wise Giving Alliance Donor Trust Report 2024
  • 66% of Millennials track results for most/all nonprofits they support — vs. 32% of Boomers. Gen Z + Millennials are 3× more likely to engage in advocacy when outcomes are visible. Boston College CCC, 2024
  • Black-led nonprofits operate with revenues 24% lower than white-led peers — a $20M median funding gap, largely because donors can't find or verify them. The African Philanthropy Forum launched Start Point Africa in 2022 explicitly to bridge this gap. Candid, 2024
  • GlobalGiving's research with IPA shows direct SMS feedback from beneficiaries measurably increases donation level and frequency — the "see the human behind the work" mechanism is validated. IPA study
Verdict — documented market gap

Visibility-as-dignity is not romantic framing. The pain is bilateral (donors can't verify; grassroots can't be seen), quantified across four independent research sources, and already generating platform responses (GlobalGiving, Start Point Africa). The generational-shift tailwind is strong — Millennials and Gen Z demand what Boomers didn't, at 2× the rate. Martin's pain maps onto measurable donor-side demand.

From Q4

7. Governance-as-feature — the training-cliff problem

Finding: Two participants offered working governance: Brian's sociocratic circle method ("that's after 2 years of sociocratic training") and Colleen's Mondragon model ("70 years' worth of experience behind it"). Everyone else was emergent/informal. Sage's read: working governance has high training cliffs or multi-decade embedding, and "good defaults alone" will drift.

Supporting data — the working models
  • Mondragon Corporation (2024): €11.2B in sales, 70,085 workers, 81 self-governing cooperatives, 14 R&D centers. Founded 1956 — Colleen's "70 years" is accurate (69 as of 2026). The News Co-op, 2024
  • Sociocracy training depth: Sociocracy For All's flagship Academy is 5 months; published practitioner accounts describe 3-month trial periods just to reach basic competency. Brian's "2 years" aligns with the curriculum's depth tiers. SoFA Academy
  • US worker co-op sector is growing slowly despite tooling: 2025 State of the Sector tracked only 242 firms; Democracy at Work Institute notes growth "has slowed considerably." USFWC Census
Supporting data — governance-as-code failures
  • Zappos Holacracy unwind: In 2015, 14% took the buyout when Holacracy rolled out; by January 2016, 18% (~260 employees) had left; 30% annual turnover (10 points above baseline). By 2020, Zappos had "quietly backed away from holacracy" and re-introduced managers. Quartz, 2019
  • Decidim civic-tech platform: Deployed at 31 cities and 13 regions. Decidim's own paper notes the platform required building a separate Metadecidim human assembly to govern itself — i.e., the software alone didn't produce democratic practice; a parallel human institution did. Computational Culture
Verdict — both claims hold

(a) Good governance defaults alone don't work — Zappos had Holacracy's full "constitution-as-code" and still hit 30% turnover plus a quiet retreat to managers; Decidim had to spin up a parallel human assembly. (b) Winning patterns carry multi-month to multi-decade embedding costs. If Mito bakes in governance patterns without a training / facilitation layer, the Wednesday group will probably probe exactly where Zappos failed.

One honest caveat

Sample bias

The session was a 90-minute interview with a self-selecting GRC subset — people who show up Wednesdays at 11 AM ET to talk regeneration. That's a specific psychographic: mission-driven, relational, anti-SaaS, under-resourced, technically curious.

The supporting data above pulls from broader populations (all US nonprofits, all knowledge workers, all donors) where it's available. That's what lets you tell nuance from systemic. Where the GRC voice and the wider data diverge, trust the wider data. And if Mito's target expands beyond the regenerative ecosystem, run the interview again with a different room.
