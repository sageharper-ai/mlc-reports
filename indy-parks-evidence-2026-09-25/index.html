Parks as Generative Infrastructure: Evidence Review

A companion to Indy Rishi Singh's Ward 7 platform · Prepared by Mindful Living · September 25, 2026

At a glance

The vision holds up. Its central legal lever is real and took effect on January 1, 2026, and several of its pieces already work nearby. The references below do two jobs: they flag pitfalls before an opponent finds them, and they show what is already possible.

Three fixes would make the platform more persuasive and precise:

  1. Date or update the statistics. The housing numbers come from 2023. The 2025 report shows a higher rent and a higher wage needed to afford it; its household counts are still 2023-based estimates.
  2. Say how the lease lever actually works. It needs a two-thirds board vote and a finding that the use is compatible with public use and enjoyment of the land. A publicly accessible food commons may qualify, subject to the board's finding. Senior housing on park land is the hardest case.
  3. Settle the stipend number. The document says $33 an hour in some places and $18 in others. The district's own program leaves the rate for bidders to propose.

Beyond the three fixes, name partners only once they've agreed, and lead with the models that already work next door. The Closing the gaps section gives a sourced way to close each open gap.

The legal lever

The 99-year lease power is real, but it belongs to the board, not to one director. The statute's operative text reads:

"Notwithstanding Sections 5540 and 5563, the Board of Directors of the Midpeninsula Regional Open Space District may, and the Board of Directors of the East Bay Regional Park District may, without obtaining the consent of the voters, lease real property for a term not exceeding 99 years. A lease entered into pursuant to this section shall be authorized by a resolution adopted by the affirmative votes of at least two-thirds of the members of the board, upon making an express finding that the purpose of the lease is for park or open-space purposes, or for an historic preservation, recreation, climate-resiliency, social equity, or agricultural purpose that is compatible with public use and enjoyment of the real property."

It was amended by AB 769 (2025), effective January 1, 2026. The Assembly analysis notes that it broadened an earlier 50-year exception and that the district sponsored the bill.

What it means for the campaign:

  • It's a board decision, not a solo power. On the seven-member board, two-thirds means at least five votes. The message becomes coalition building: "I'll build support for proposals that meet the legal test."
  • "Compatible with public use and enjoyment" is the test. A publicly accessible food forest, orchard or farm may qualify, subject to the board's express finding and site review. A residential care facility that closes land to the public is the hardest case. Present it as exploratory, or site it on adjacent non-park land.
  • We found no published lease under this section yet. Ask the district; if none exists, using it well first is a strong story.

Proven elsewhere

Every major piece of the platform already works somewhere nearby, which is the strongest answer to "that's just a dream."

  • Public food-forest park: Atlanta's Urban Food Forest at Browns Mill is edible landscape on public park land.
  • Public utility land for food: AgLantis farms leased sanitation-district land with recycled water.
  • Farm and job training: Family Harvest Farm employs foster youth.
  • Prescribed produce: Recipe4Health, with Stanford's evaluation.
  • Resident budgeting: Vallejo participatory budgeting, running since 2012.
  • Fuel to soil: the district's own carbonizer pilot and, separately, the Lake County carbonizer.

Closing the gaps

Each gap below has a practical way to close it and the sources to start from. These are options for the campaign, not commitments by any organization named.

1. Identify tribal governments and groups to consult.

  • Groups to ask:
    • Indigenous-led groups active in East Bay land stewardship include the Confederated Villages of Lisjan and the Sogorea Te' Land Trust, which secured an Oakland cultural conservation easement.
    • The mak-warép Ohlone Land Conservancy reports cultural burning on a 100-acre site in Concord.
    • The Muwekma Ohlone Tribe is another group to approach through its own channels.
    • Ask the district and local Native communities which Bay Miwok groups should also be consulted.
  • What the district already has: it consulted Lisjan and Muwekma on the future Golden Gate Fields park and signed a Point Molate letter of intent with Guidiville Rancheria. Neither is a cultural-burning agreement.
  • How to approach: go through each group's own channels, bring a specific proposal and budget, and let them set the terms, including whether to be named. mak-warép says it works on its own terms.

2. Bring labor in before setting numbers.

  • Invite AFSCME Local 2428, the district's employee bargaining unit, and field staff to review workload, wages, training and any stipend program.
  • In a July 2026 announcement, the district said the former Maintenance and Skilled Trades division lead had overseen 107 staff. That describes one division, not a districtwide staffing or vacancy figure.

3. Match each piece to a real funding source. None of these is a blanket source for everything; each needs its own eligibility check.

4. Frame money and upkeep accurately.

  • The $35.175M in 2024 notes financed renovating 2955 Peralta Oaks Court for police, fire and administrative staff (2024 audit, p. 63).
  • The stronger upkeep facts are these:
    • the district reported over $89 million in deferred facility maintenance (a 2021-era figure)
    • its 2026 proposed budget estimates $11 million a year for infrastructure repair and renovation
  • Cite each with its date. Don't link the notes to staffing as cause and effect.

5. Set a few 2030 targets on the district's own baselines.

  • Fuels: build on the district's claim of over 500 acres treated a year and its completed 667-acre Anthony Chabot project.
  • Access and habitat: align with the district's District Plan now in development.
  • Pick three measures and report them every year: acres treated, paid trainee placements, and acres opened to public food or habitat use.
  • Specific quantities need site and budget work first.

6. Swap jargon for plain words. Confirm each meaning with the candidate first.

  • "Arrest Disorder" → "prevent damage and help land recover"
  • "psychoneuroimmunology" → "how stress, the brain and the immune system affect health"
  • "Eight Forms of Capital" → "the resources a community can build: money, skills, relationships, culture and healthy land"

Evolving the message

The public spine is strong as written: Healthy land. Healthy people. Public money that works. Each part can now carry proof:

  • Healthy land: "The district asked Sacramento for 99-year leasing power, and got it this year. I'll build support for qualifying food commons the public can walk into."
  • Healthy people: "Food as medicine already works next door. Stanford found Alameda's Recipe4Health improved patients' cholesterol."
  • Public money that works: "Since 2012, Vallejo residents have proposed and recommended projects for real city dollars. Ward 7 can too."
  • Fire: "The district already turned removed vegetation into biochar in a 2022–23 pilot. I'll push to scale it."

Questions for Indy

  1. Which named partners have agreed to be named?
  2. Which tribal nations or groups is the plan built with, and have they been asked?
  3. What is the stipend rate, and where does the money come from?
  4. Would the senior care campus sit on park buffer land or next to it?
  5. Which parcels come first, and how would they reach five board votes?
  6. What would success look like by 2030, in acres, people and dollars?
