What's wrong with the current pages

Both reviewer rounds passed v6.4, and the Founder still rejected it. So the review lens was the problem, not the wording. It checked accuracy and sanded the voice off.

  • Hedges: the approved home page uses about 0.2 per 100 words ("could", "may", "designed to", "explores", "proposal"). The leg pages use 0.9 (Acting), 1.8 (Seeing) and 3.2 (Governing).
  • Questions: the home page has none. Each leg page has 8 to 10, including question headlines ("What could happen when…?").
  • Weaker than the parent: the home page already says these things harder than the leg pages do ("You can't fix what you can't find", "without wearing anyone out", "decide, own, hand over"). The leg pages dropped those lines instead of deepening them.
  • Abstract: "a community", "shared step", "occasions have thinned". No scene, no place, no person.

New rule for these drafts: say each status once, plainly, the way the home does ("In development, not yet shipped"). Everything else is declarative.

See your place clearly.

You can't fix what you can't find. A community needs a living picture of its people, its assets, its real needs and the potential sitting in plain sight. It should be true enough to act on, and owned by the people in it.

The places that used to show a town to itself are closing.

Local papers told a community who was trying what, and what got decided. A third of them have closed since 2005, and fifty-five million Americans now live where almost no one reports on their own place. The work didn't stop; it stopped being visible. So groups in the same town duplicate each other, miss each other, and leave help sitting idle.

Seen from the inside, not the top down.

A picture drawn from above can be used on a community instead of by it. Ours starts with one person keeping a journal of their own work. Many journals, shared by choice, let a community see itself. What each person records stays theirs to share or keep.

How it works today

  1. Start a journal. Record what you're working on and what you're learning.
  2. Tell us what you're working on. We'll help you find who else is on it, and what they've tried.
  3. Share what you choose. As more people do, it adds up to a shared picture: who's doing what, where efforts overlap, what's sitting idle, what's missing.

The journal is a working prototype. The shared picture is being designed with its first users.

A · Home, continued

Act together, without wearing anyone out.

Communities already know how to act. What's usually missing is the coordination: who's moving on what, what the next step is, and who's carrying too much. That's the part that rarely survives a budget.

The work nobody funds.

Even when a dozen groups want to work together, the coordination itself is the one thing almost no grant will pay for. So good work runs separately, on borrowed time, until the people carrying it wear out.

Start with what people already want to do.

Talk it through with neighbors, draft a proposal, try it small. We're building tools that make the shared next step the easy one to take, and that keep the work visible so no one carries it alone. The community runs them, not us.

How it works

  1. Name one shared step. Small, and something the people involved care about.
  2. Split the work where everyone can see it.
  3. Report back and build on it, including what didn't work.

In development, not yet shipped.

C · Your Tuesday

The meeting went well. Then what?

Everyone agreed. Ideas on the whiteboard, energy in the room. Three weeks later, two people are doing all of it, and the rest aren't sure what happened.

It isn't a people problem.

It's a coordination problem, and coordination is the work nobody funds. So it falls on whoever cares most, until they burn out.

What helps

Pick one small shared step. Split it so everyone can see who's doing what. Tell everyone what happened, including what didn't work, and choose the next step from there. It's simple, and it's hard to keep up by hand.

What we're building

Tools that make that loop the easy path: the next step visible, the work shared, the record kept. Your community runs them; we hand them over. In development, not yet shipped.

Governing: four directions

Now (v6.4): "What could happen when a community owns what it builds?" What a community builds raises two questions: who owns it, and who decides what happens next? Our proposal explores how…

Jump to: A · B · C · D

A · Home, continued

Govern in the open: decide, own, hand over.

How a place decides, owns and hands things on. Agreements built to outlast any founder, windfall or crisis, so the future answers to the people who live there.

The handoff with no heir.

A generation of owners (shops, firms, farms) is reaching retirement with no successor in sight. When a business closes or sells out of town, the ownership never comes back. The same thing happens to a community project when its founder leaves or its grant ends.

Written down, and practiced first on our own work.

Communities already govern themselves; we don't replace that. We publish how decisions get made, how ownership is held and how things pass on, as we practice it, for any community to take and run. The destination is community cooperatives: contribution earns standing, participation earns ownership.

How it works, as proposed

  1. Agree who decides, and how, before the work begins.
  2. Keep a record anyone can check.
  3. Agree how what's built is held, and who carries it on when people leave.

A published proposal. Read it and push back.
